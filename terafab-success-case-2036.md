# Terafab by 2036: The Grand Success Case for Tesla and SpaceX

*A speculative strategic analysis inspired by the Herbert Ong / Joe Baggett discussion of Intel joining Terafab, Space AI economics, and vertical integration at unlimited scale ([YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=rOQKE1vT56w)).*

---

## Part I — Three Detailed ROI Breakeven Scenarios by 2036

Assumptions common to all scenarios:

- Phase 1 capital: ~$16.8B (Tesla + SpaceX).
- Multi-phase cumulative capital through 2036: $55B–$119B depending on buildout pace.
- “Breakeven” = cumulative Terafab operating contribution (captive cost avoided + external packaging/fab revenue + strategic margin retained vs. foundry pricing) recovers cumulative invested capital by year-end 2036.
- Captive demand comes from vehicles, Optimus, Cybercab, Starlink / orbital AI nodes, and terrestrial / space data-center silicon.
- Packaging and test lead; front-end wafer fab and FEL-EUV mature mid-decade; merchant services fill spare capacity.

---

### Scenario A — “Captive Inferno”: Internal Demand Alone Pays Back Capex

**Thesis:** Cars, Optimus, and SpaceX orbital compute absorb essentially all Terafab output. External sales are optional. ROI breakeven arrives because every wafer not bought from TSMC/Samsung at scarcity premiums is a dollar kept inside the Musk ecosystem.

**Capital & capacity path**

- 2026–2027: Giga Texas advanced technology prototype fab live; few thousand wafers/month; packaging/test lines qualify AI5/AI6-class modules.
- 2028–2029: Grimes County Phase 1 packaging campus at high utilization; limited Intel 14A-licensed pilot logic; memory stacking and multi-chip modules under one roof.
- 2030–2032: Front-end logic + memory lines ramp; FEL EUV pilot beams online; wafer starts climb toward hundreds of thousands per month.
- 2033–2036: Toward ~1M wafer starts/month peak ambition; cumulative capital ~$80–$100B; utilization sustained by captive pull.

**Demand drivers that force full absorption**

- Vehicle fleet: high-volume FSD inference silicon per car (multiple redundant SoCs) across Model line refresh + Cybercab ramp; millions of units/year by early 2030s.
- Optimus: inference packs, motor controllers, vision stacks; even partial progress toward tens of millions of robots implies billions of specialized chips and packages.
- SpaceX orbital AI / Starlink-class nodes: radiation-tolerant, thermally extreme, inference-first ASICs at constellation scale; Musk’s public framing that terrestrial foundries can supply only a few percent of needed compute.
- Avoided foundry premium: if captive silicon saves $200–$800 per high-end module at millions–billions of units, annual avoided spend alone is multi-billion.

**Economics that hit breakeven by 2036**

- Cumulative avoided external foundry + OSAT spend (2028–2036) exceeds cumulative Terafab cash invested in the base case (~$70–$90B of avoided cost vs. ~$80–$100B invested).
- Iteration speed (mask revise → fab → package → test on one campus) shortens AI chip cycles from quarters to weeks, raising product margin on cars/robots/satellites enough to attribute incremental gross profit to Terafab.
- Tax abatements (e.g., multi-year Grimes County property relief through mid-2030s) lower holding cost during ramp.
- No dependence on merchant foundry customers; spare capacity is inventory buffer for Optimus/Cybercab spikes, not idle risk.

**Breakeven markers (bullet checklist)**

- Packaging/test cash-flow positive by 2029 on captive modules alone.
- Front-end logic contribution margin positive by 2031 on AI7-class nodes.
- Cumulative NPV of avoided TSMC/Samsung spend crosses cumulative CapEx by 2035–2036.
- Fleet + Optimus + orbital node silicon volumes grow ≥30% CAGR through 2034.
- Yield on advanced packages exceeds 90% by 2032; FEL-assisted litho cuts EUV cost/wafer ~50% vs. LPP baseline by 2034.

**Why this is a “grand success” for Tesla and SpaceX**

- Tesla is no longer a car company waiting in a foundry queue; it owns the inference transistor supply that Autopilot, Cybercab, and Optimus require.
- SpaceX can schedule orbital AI launches against known silicon output rather than against Taiwan allocation politics.
- Combined entity (or deep JV) becomes a strategic U.S. compute asset—echoing the Herbert discussion that these firms are becoming national infrastructure, not mere equities.

---

### Scenario B — “OSAT Supernova”: Packaging & Test as Merchant Profit Engine

**Thesis:** Terafab’s first decade is dominated by advanced packaging and test—the true bottleneck of the AI age. Captive demand fills the floor; selling CoWoS-class / EMIB-like packaging and burn-in to third parties fills the ceiling. Merchant packaging revenue, not wafer starts, drives earlier ROI.

**Capital & capacity path**

- 2026–2028: Heavy CapEx into assembly, HBM attach, substrate, thermal, and system-level test; lighter CapEx into front-end until yields and FEL readiness justify it.
- 2029–2031: Merchant packaging contracts with U.S. hyperscalers, defense primes, and select AI chip startups starved of Taiwan OSAT slots.
- 2032–2036: Front-end ramps behind packaging; FEL + Intel process maturity enable captive logic; packaging remains the highest-ROI floor of the campus.

**Revenue & margin mechanics**

- Advanced packaging ASP and margins historically rival or exceed many front-end nodes under scarcity; Terafab monetizes that scarcity on U.S. soil.
- Captive Tesla/SpaceX modules pay “internal transfer” that still books as contribution vs. external OSAT quotes.
- Third-party packaging at 20–40% of line capacity by 2032 can add several billion dollars/year of high-margin revenue without waiting for leading-edge wafer parity with TSMC.
- Testing (wafer sort, known-good-die, burn-in for space-grade parts) is sticky, recurring, and pairs with SpaceX radiation-hardening know-how as a differentiator.

**Demand coupling**

- Cars and Optimus still soak captive lines—ensuring utilization even if merchant demand softens.
- Data-center AI boom (terrestrial + early orbital) keeps external packaging booked years ahead.
- Intel partnership accelerates EMIB / multi-die know-how transfer into Terafab’s packaging halls, shortening learning curves.

**Breakeven markers (bullet checklist)**

- Packaging campus utilization ≥85% from 2029 onward (captive + merchant mix).
- Merchant packaging + test revenue ≥$8–$15B/year by 2033 at 35%+ contribution margins.
- Cumulative packaging/test free cash flow (2028–2036) covers Phase 1 ($16.8B) by ~2031 and full multi-phase CapEx by 2035–2036 when combined with captive avoided cost.
- External customer backlog ≥24 months by 2030; multi-year take-or-pay agreements de-risk CapEx.
- Space-grade test services command 2–3× terrestrial premiums, lifting blended margins.
- Front-end remains a “second act” that arrives already partially funded by packaging profits.

**Why this is a “grand success”**

- Matches the Herbert/Bag insight that Elon’s edge is economics and scale, not beating Nvidia at general GPU design: own the scarce step (package/test at insane volume).
- Turns Terafab into the Western answer to advanced packaging concentration in Asia—strategic and profitable.
- Funds FEL and front-end learning with cash from a business that works before perfect lithography exists.

### Scenario C — “FEL Leapfrog + Dual Orbit”: Technology Cost Curve + Dual Markets

**Thesis:** xLight-class / Intel-aligned FEL EUV R&D matures into production light sources by ~2030, cutting EUV cost ~50% and wafer cost ~20%, while doubling effective throughput at scale. Combined with explosive dual demand—edge inference (cars/Optimus) and space AI data centers—unit economics flip so hard that even a $100B+ CapEx stack breaks even by 2036.

**Technology sequence**

- 2026–2028: Prototype fab + packaging; conventional EUV/DUV where needed; FEL R&D partnership (xLight, Intel process teams, possibly Tau/Inversion talent) on Terafab’s long-axis / accelerator footprint.
- 2028–2030: FEL pilot beamlines feed lithography cells; Intel 14A-class process maturity; first production wafers with hybrid LPP+FEL or FEL-primary sources.
- 2031–2034: FEL primary EUV at volume; lower power, higher dose, better uptime vs. tin-plasma LPP; wafer cost advantage compounds across millions of starts.
- 2035–2036: Cost leadership on inference-optimized nodes (not necessarily beating Nvidia training GPUs feature-for-feature) at scales competitors cannot match.

**Dual-market pull**

- **Earth edge:** every car and robot is a recurring silicon customer; software improvements increase silicon attach rates over time.
- **Orbit:** once Starship $/kg collapses, space AI becomes cheaper than many terrestrial data centers (the Baggett “way bigger and way sooner” thesis); each orbital node needs radiation-tolerant, thermally specialized silicon that Terafab uniquely co-designs with SpaceX.
- Shared architecture: inference-first ASICs with packaging variants for automotive, humanoid, and vacuum/radiation environments—amortizing design NRE across three SKUs of one platform family.

**Economics that hit breakeven by 2036**

- Cost/wafer advantage of ~15–25% vs. conventional EUV fabs widens contribution margin on captive and selective merchant logic.
- Cumulative CapEx toward the high end (~$100–$119B) is offset by:
  - avoided foundry spend at megascale,
  - merchant packaging cash flows,
  - lower opex from FEL efficiency and Texas energy/tax structure,
  - attributed profit from Cybercab/Optimus/orbital AI products that could not ship without captive silicon.
- Strategic premium: U.S. government and hyperscaler interest in secure domestic advanced packaging + FEL lithography creates long-term offtake and potential co-funding that improves effective ROI.

**Breakeven markers (bullet checklist)**

- FEL prototype light delivered to litho tool by 2028; production qualification 2029–2030.
- Demonstrated ≥2× EUV useful power and ~50% EUV cost reduction vs. LPP baseline by 2032.
- Wafer starts: tens of thousands/month by 2030 → hundreds of thousands by 2033 → approach million-class ambition late decade.
- Blended captive + merchant gross margin on silicon modules ≥40% by 2034.
- Cumulative economic value (avoided cost + external revenue + incremental product GM) ≥ cumulative invested capital by YE 2036.
- Space AI silicon share of Terafab output rises from minority (late 2020s) to plurality (mid-2030s) as orbital constellations densify.
- Cars/Optimus provide the “base load” that keeps lines running during orbital deployment cadence gaps.

**Why this is a “grand success”**

- Leapfrogs the industry playbook (Musk’s “FEL FTW”) instead of renting ASML’s status quo forever.
- Makes Terafab the physical embodiment of Kardashev-scale ambition discussed in the Herbert episode: unlimited cheap inference silicon as the bottleneck unlock for robots and space compute.
- Positions Tesla/SpaceX as the only entities that can co-optimize chips, robots, vehicles, rockets, and orbital power as one closed economic loop.

## Part II — Essay: How the Grand Success Case Plays Out (~3,000 words)

		The story of Terafab’s success is not the story of a car company dabbling in semiconductors. It is the story of two organizations—Tesla and SpaceX—discovering that the binding constraint on their futures is not battery chemistry, not Falcon cadence, not even the next neural-net architecture, but the physical availability of inference silicon at civilization scale. In the Herbert Ong conversation with economist Joe Baggett, that constraint is named without apology: Elon’s magic is economics, and the winning move is to optimize for unlimited chips at reasonable cost rather than for beating Nvidia at every niche of general-purpose GPU design. By 2036, if the path sketched below unfolds, Terafab will have repaid its extraordinary capital outlay not because Wall Street cheered a fab IRR model in 2027, but because cars, humanoids, and orbital data centers created a demand vortex that no merchant foundry could have filled—and because Terafab learned to crawl with packaging, walk with merchant assembly, and run with free-electron-laser lithography before the decade closed.

		Begin with the honest starting position of 2026. Terafab is announced as the most epic chip-building effort on Earth: logic, memory, and advanced packaging under one roof; a prototype advanced technology fab rising on Giga Texas’s north campus; a full-scale complex sited in Grimes County with an initial capital pulse on the order of $16.8 billion and a multi-phase horizon that filings and commentary stretch toward $55 billion and even $119 billion. Intel joins, not as a press-release ornament, but as the American process and packaging partner whose 14A-class roadmap and multi-die expertise can compress decades of foundry learning into a joint program. Critics call the campus a fantasy. Supporters answer with a chart that is almost rude in its clarity: what the global industry can supply is a few percent of what Tesla and SpaceX say they will need for Optimus, Cybercab, and space-based AI. Baggett’s framing in the Herbert discussion fits here exactly—this is not about winning Broadcom’s socket wars; it is about manufacturing at a scale no one else is even attempting, for products whose designs are already inference-specific. The strategic grammar is vertical integration plus recursive speed: own the bottleneck, shorten the feedback loop, and let product volume amortize mistakes that would bankrupt a pure-play foundry chasing every customer’s wishlist.

		The first act is deliberately humble: packaging and testing. Modern AI silicon is a multi-die creature. The wafer is necessary; the package is often decisive. CoWoS-class capacity, HBM attach, substrate warpage, thermal interfaces, known-good-die logistics, and system-level burn-in are the chokepoints that turn beautiful die into shippable modules. Terafab’s earliest cash-generating identity, therefore, is an onshore advanced OSAT fused to Tesla and SpaceX design teams. In 2026 and 2027, the Austin prototype line does what no distributed supply chain can do cleanly: make a chip-ish assembly, test it, revise the mask or the substrate stack, and repeat without FedExing wafers across oceans. AI5 and its successors become the forced curriculum. Yield engineers learn automotive functional safety and space radiation screening in the same building. Finance learns that every module packaged internally is a module not waiting in a Taiwan queue at a scarcity premium. By late 2027 and into 2028, as Grimes County’s first halls energize, the packaging floors are not empty monuments—they are booked by captive demand from vehicle inference boards and early Optimus torsos, with test bays already qualifying parts destined for harsh orbits. This is the crawl phase that looks unglamorous in press photos and decisive on the income statement: cash begins to move before the particle accelerator ever sings.

		That captive floor is what makes merchant packaging possible without existential risk. Once utilization is anchored by Tesla and SpaceX, spare capacity becomes a product. Hyperscalers and AI chip companies that cannot secure advanced packaging slots discover a Texas campus willing to sell assembly and test under multi-year take-or-pay contracts. Intel’s fingerprints matter: EMIB-like bridging, American process discipline, and a political story that U.S. policymakers can understand. Terafab does not need to become TSMC in 2029; it needs to become the most important advanced packaging and test node in the Western Hemisphere. In Scenario B’s spirit, this merchant layer can throw off enough high-margin cash by the early 2030s that Phase 1 capital is effectively retired from packaging profits alone, while front-end ambitions ride on a balance sheet already warmed by assembly revenue. The Herbert interview’s insistence that Terafab is “ultimately easier” than FSD or Starship is controversial as engineering poetry—but as sequencing strategy it is shrewd: start where the bottleneck and the margin already live. Offering to package other companies’ chips is not a distraction from the mission; it is how the mission learns at someone else’s volume while still prioritizing the parents’ silicon in every allocation meeting.

		All along, demand is not a flat line waiting for a fab to turn on. It is a rising storm with three fronts that reinforce one another. The first front is the car. Every advance in FSD and autonomy increases the silicon content per vehicle and the redundancy required for safety. Cybercab, if it scales as Musk projects, is not a trim level; it is a silicon appliance on wheels, refreshed on software timescales that punish long foundry lead times. Fleet growth compounds attach rates: more cars, more cameras, more inference boards, more spare modules in the service channel. The second front is Optimus. Humanoid robots are often debated as science projects; Terafab’s planners must treat them as a bill of materials. Cameras, joint controllers, inference packs, safety islands—multiplied by millions and then tens of millions of units—turn “a few chips per robot” into an industrial tide. Even a slower Optimus ramp than the bull case still creates a base-load of heterogeneous packages that keep lines busy between vehicle model years. The third front is data-center infrastructure, first terrestrial and then orbital. Baggett’s claim on the Herbert show—that once Starship’s cost per kilogram collapses, space AI becomes cheaper than many Earth data centers “next year” in projection terms—may be early or late by a few years, but the direction is the strategic hinge. If orbital compute is real, then radiation-tolerant, thermally specialized, inference-first chips are not a niche; they are the long pole of the tent. Terafab exists to fabricate that pole in volumes the merchant market will not prioritize. Crucially, these three fronts do not compete for a fixed pie of wafers so much as they share architectures: an inference family with packaging variants for asphalt, factory floors, and vacuum. Shared NRE is the quiet multiplier that makes a captive fab look rational on a spreadsheet.

		The second act is the maturation of light itself. For most of the industry, extreme ultraviolet lithography means ASML’s laser-produced plasma source: tin droplets, pulsed lasers, collectors that foul, power that is expensive. Musk’s “FEL FTW” reply, and the particle-accelerator geometry teased in Terafab renderings, point at a different physics: free-electron lasers that undulate relativistic electrons into coherent EUV, potentially delivering several times the useful power, cutting EUV cost roughly in half, and trimming total wafer cost on the order of twenty percent, with throughput and yield upside if the beam proves manufacturable. xLight, chaired by former Intel CEO Pat Gelsinger, and peer efforts at firms like Tau Systems and Inversion Semiconductor, sit on this frontier. Intel’s presence inside Terafab creates a natural diplomatic and technical bridge: process recipes, lithography integration, and the institutional memory of what it takes to bring a radical light source from prototype to uptime percentages that CFOs will fund. Between 2028 and 2030, the speculative but coherent path is pilot beamlines feeding real scanners—still coexisting with conventional sources—while packaging profits and captive module demand pay the electric bills. By 2030, if qualification holds, Terafab begins to fab leading logic and memory not as a me-too clone of Hsinchu, but as a cost-advantaged inference foundry whose light source was designed for a building large enough to host an accelerator. Maturing xLight’s and Intel’s R&D into production FEL tech is therefore less a moonshot bolted onto a factory and more the factory’s reason for being shaped like a moonshot: the campus was sized for physics that ordinary fabs cannot host.

		Front-end success in this telling is not vanity. It is the closing of the loop that packaging opened. Once FEL-assisted nodes and Intel-licensed processes can produce AI7-class and later dies at acceptable yield, the campus stops being “an OSAT with a research cleanroom” and becomes what the announcement always claimed: logic, memory, and packaging recursive under one roof. Design teams iterate in days. Memory stacks tuned for Optimus’s power envelope ship beside radiation-hardened variants for SpaceXAI orbital nodes. The same mask set family branches into automotive AEC-Q qualification and space screening. Vertical integration here is not a slogan from a 1990s PC company; it is a control-system for learning rate. Apple taught the industry what happens when software and silicon share a P&L. Terafab teaches what happens when silicon, robots, cars, and rockets share a civilization roadmap. The walk-to-run transition around 2030 is the moment outsiders finally see “a fab.” Insiders will know the fab was already profitable as a packaging organism; the front end simply stopped renting transistors from strangers.

		Money, in the Baggett sense, is the plot. Cumulative capital toward the high end looks absurd until it is compared with the absurdity of the demand chart. If Optimus, Cybercab, and orbital AI are even partially right, then paying foundry scarcity prices forever is the true fantasy. Scenario A’s breakeven math is brutal and simple: avoided external spend plus attributed product gross margin from products that only ship because silicon exists can repay tens of billions by 2036 without needing a famous merchant foundry brand. Scenario B’s math is smoother: packaging and test as a cash engine, merchant contracts as de-risking, front-end as a funded second engine. Scenario C’s math is technological: FEL cost curves turn every wafer into a wider contribution margin just as volumes explode. The grand success case is the braid of all three—captive absorption, merchant packaging, and lithography leapfrog—rather than a single hero metric. Year by year, the braid tightens. In 2028, avoided OSAT fees and early module shipments matter most. In 2031, merchant packaging backlog and FEL pilot yields share the spotlight. In 2034, wafer-cost deltas and orbital constellation densification dominate the CFO’s dashboard. By 2036, the cumulative column labeled “economic value created by owning the stack” crosses the cumulative column labeled “cash poured into Texas concrete and tools.” That crossing is ROI breakeven in the only sense that matters for owners who also own the demand.

		Watch how the demand engines specifically feed that crossing. Vehicle sales and Autonomy attach rates create a predictable, regulation-shaped cadence: model years, hardware revisions, dual-redundant compute for safety cases. That cadence is Terafab’s metronome—the rhythm that keeps cleanrooms staffed and depreciation schedules honest. Optimus injects bursts: factory deployments inside Tesla and SpaceX first, then external customers, each robot a cluster of packages that must be tested like mission hardware because a humanoid failure mode is not a blue screen—it is a physical event. Data-center infrastructure—racks on Earth, then buses in sun-synchronous orbit—injects the asymptotic hunger Baggett describes as “way bigger and way sooner.” When Starship’s marginal cost of mass falls, the rational response of capital is not to debate aesthetics of orbital compute; it is to order silicon. Terafab’s job is to have that silicon waiting, already screened for radiation and already packaged for thermal extremes that Earth servers never see. Growth of sales in cars, Optimus, and data-center infra does not merely “support” the fab; it is the fab’s product-market fit, renewed every quarter.

		A decade-long playbook clarifies the handoffs. From 2026 to 2028, the mandate is learning velocity: qualify packages, own test recipes, prove that one-roof iteration beats a globe-spanning logistics graph. From 2029 to 2031, the mandate is cash and credibility: sell spare packaging capacity, lock take-or-pay contracts, and let Intel-aligned process transfers populate the pilot front end without pretending the pilot is already Hsinchu. From 2030 to 2033, the mandate is light and wafers: bring FEL from R&D theater into production uptime, migrate AI7-class and successor dies onto captive flows, and publish internal cost curves that justify the next CapEx tranche. From 2034 to 2036, the mandate is scale and payback: push wafer starts toward the ambitious end of the roadmap, let orbital AI densification absorb the long-tail of specialized silicon, and book the cumulative avoided cost and merchant profit that retire the investment. Each phase leaves assets the next phase inherits—tooling, talent, customer contracts, and political goodwill—so that breakeven is not a single lucky year but the integral of a deliberately staged curve.

		Governance and geopolitics thicken the plot without needing melodrama. The Herbert conversation dwells on Tesla and SpaceX as strategic assets: Intel’s American partnership, Starship’s implications for power projection, the intuition that mega-corporations are absorbing functions once reserved for states. Terafab sits at that intersection. A domestic campus that can package advanced AI modules and, later, fab them with a next-generation light source is not only a Tesla supply chain hedge; it is a national capability argument. Tax abatements in Grimes County through the mid-2030s, Texas Enterprise funding, and the employment of thousands are the local politics. The deeper politics is priority: when the world’s leading edge is concentrated offshore, the entity that can guarantee inference silicon for autonomous fleets and orbital constellations holds leverage that compounds. Whether or not Tesla and SpaceX formally merge—as Chamath’s near-certainty and Baggett’s 99.99% aside suggest many expect—the fab already behaves like shared nervous tissue. Boards argue about transfer prices; engineers argue about mask revisions; the civilization roadmap does not wait for the argument to end.

		Risks remain real, and a success essay that ignores them is marketing. Semiconductor equipment ages; utilization must be high early or CapEx becomes a trap. FEL may slip past 2030. Intel’s process may mature slower than slides promise. Optimus may scale later than keynotes. Orbital data centers may face thermal, debris, or regulatory walls. Merchant customers may fear co-locating with a competitor’s captive lines. Yield excursions can erase a year of margin. Talent wars with TSMC, Samsung, and ASML can slow every ramp. The success case does not deny these; it sequences around them. Packaging first reduces technology risk. Captive demand reduces utilization risk. Merchant take-or-pay reduces financing risk. FEL as a mid-decade amplifier reduces cost risk after the campus already exists. Cars and robots provide base load when launch cadences wobble. Space AI provides upside when Earth edge saturates a given node. Diversified SKUs inside one inference family reduce the odds that a single product delay idles a hundred million square feet. This is not optimism as mood; it is optimism as portfolio construction inside one factory.

		By the early 2030s, the narrative inside Tesla and SpaceX communications can shift from “we must build Terafab or we will not have chips” to “Terafab is why we can quote Cybercab and Optimus volumes that frighten incumbents.” Quarterly letters speak of module costs falling on FEL learning curves. SpaceX schedules satellite AI buses against known wafer starts. xAI training and inference workloads—whether branded under SpaceXAI or adjacent—pull specialized silicon that never needed to be a universal GPU. Competitors scramble for leftover OSAT capacity and discover that the scarce resource was not only EUV scanners but the willingness to build a hundred-million-square-foot answer to a demand curve everyone else treated as rhetoric. Analysts who once modeled Terafab as a dilutive science project begin modeling it as a margin expander on every robot shipped and every orbital watt activated. The Herbert episode’s larger point lands with force: these companies are no longer “just” equity stories; they are becoming infrastructure through which energy, transport, AI, chips, and space run. Terafab is the chip chapter of that infrastructure novel.

		Arrive, then, at 2036. Cumulative investment has been enormous. Cumulative value is larger. In the captive-inferno reading, Terafab has paid for itself by substituting for a foundry industry that could not have delivered anyway. In the OSAT-supernova reading, packaging and test have been a profit center so robust that lithography R&D felt like it was funded by a separate company that happened to share a roof. In the FEL-leapfrog reading, the cost of light itself bent, and inference wafers became the cheap commodity on which robots and orbital watts were built. The compelling case is that all three readings are chapters of one book. Terafab grew up by packaging and testing AI chips for its parents; it earned its keep by packaging chips for others; it matured xLight’s and Intel’s FEL and process R&D into real fab capacity around 2030; and every year the sales of cars, the production of Optimus, and the buildout of data-center infrastructure—on Earth and above it—tightened the demand spiral. Herbert’s guests were right that this is bigger than a car ticker and sooner than skeptics want to believe. By 2036, Terafab is not a bet that Tesla and SpaceX placed on semiconductors. It is the reason those companies could become what they claimed: engines of abundant inference, at planetary and orbital scale, with the economics finally matching the ambition.
