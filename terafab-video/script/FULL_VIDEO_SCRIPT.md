## Scene 20_essay_demand: Essay — Three Demand Fronts

**On-screen bullets:**
- Cars / Cybercab silicon appliances
- Optimus bill of materials at scale
- Data centers → orbital AI
- Shared inference family + NRE

**Narration:**

All along, demand is not a flat line waiting for a fab to turn on. It is a rising storm with three fronts that reinforce one another.
The first front is the car. Every advance in F S D and autonomy increases the silicon content per vehicle and the redundancy required for safety. Cybercab, if it scales as Musk projects, is not a trim level; it is a silicon appliance on wheels, refreshed on software timescales that punish long foundry lead times. Fleet growth compounds attach rates: more cars, more cameras, more inference boards, more spare modules in the service channel.
The second front is Optimus. Humanoid robots are often debated as science projects; Terafab’s planners must treat them as a bill of materials. Cameras, joint controllers, inference packs, safety islands — multiplied by millions and then tens of millions of units — turn a few chips per robot into an industrial tide. Even a slower Optimus ramp than the bull case still creates a base-load of heterogeneous packages that keep lines busy between vehicle model years.
The third front is data-center infrastructure, first terrestrial and then orbital. Baggett’s claim on the Herbert show — that once Starship’s cost per kilogram collapses, space A I becomes cheaper than many Earth data centers next year in projection terms — may be early or late by a few years, but the direction is the strategic hinge. If orbital compute is real, then radiation-tolerant, thermally specialized, inference-first chips are not a niche; they are the long pole of the tent. Terafab exists to fabricate that pole in volumes the merchant market will not prioritize.
Crucially, these three fronts do not compete for a fixed pie of wafers so much as they share architectures: an inference family with packaging variants for asphalt, factory floors, and vacuum. Shared N R E is the quiet multiplier that makes a captive fab look rational on a spreadsheet.

## Scene 21_essay_fel: Essay — Act II: Maturation of Light

**On-screen bullets:**
- FEL FTW vs LPP tin plasma
- ~4× power / ~50% EUV cost
- xLight + Gelsinger + Intel bridge
- Campus sized for accelerator physics

**Narration:**

The second act is the maturation of light itself. For most of the industry, extreme ultraviolet lithography means A S M L’s laser-produced plasma source: tin droplets, pulsed lasers, collectors that foul, power that is expensive.
Musk’s “F E L for the win” reply, and the particle-accelerator geometry teased in Terafab renderings, point at a different physics: free-electron lasers that undulate relativistic electrons into coherent E U V, potentially delivering several times the useful power, cutting E U V cost roughly in half, and trimming total wafer cost on the order of twenty percent, with throughput and yield upside if the beam proves manufacturable.
xLight, chaired by former Intel C E O Pat Gelsinger, and peer efforts at firms like Tau Systems and Inversion Semiconductor, sit on this frontier.
Intel’s presence inside Terafab creates a natural diplomatic and technical bridge: process recipes, lithography integration, and the institutional memory of what it takes to bring a radical light source from prototype to uptime percentages that C F Os will fund.
Between twenty twenty-eight and twenty thirty, the speculative but coherent path is pilot beamlines feeding real scanners — still coexisting with conventional sources — while packaging profits and captive module demand pay the electric bills.
By twenty thirty, if qualification holds, Terafab begins to fab leading logic and memory not as a me-too clone of Hsinchu, but as a cost-advantaged inference foundry whose light source was designed for a building large enough to host an accelerator.
Maturing xLight’s and Intel’s R and D into production free-electron-laser tech is therefore less a moonshot bolted onto a factory and more the factory’s reason for being shaped like a moonshot: the campus was sized for physics that ordinary fabs cannot host.
